Research

Soft Earth Geophysics

Much of the ground beneath our feet, and the ice above it, behaves like soft matter: it flows, creeps, jams and fails. We bring geophysical problems into the lab, from capsizing icebergs to the slow creep of sand, and combine experiments with continuum models and field data from our collaborators to connect grain-scale physics to the scale of glaciers and landscapes.

Glaciers, icebergs and ice mélange

Polar ice plays a critical but poorly understood role in global climate. Antarctica’s and Greenland’s ice sheets are thinning and flowing toward the ocean faster than ever, and nearly half of Greenland’s mass loss happens through iceberg calving. Traditionally viewed as a slow, steady flow, ice can also fail catastrophically: ice shelves have collapsed within weeks, and a single capsizing iceberg can release energy comparable to kilotons of TNT.

Capsizing icebergs and glacial earthquakes

Our laboratory capsize experiments showed that only a small fraction of the energy released by a capsizing iceberg goes into its own motion or into surface waves; most of it is transferred directly into the surrounding water. Working with glaciologists and seismologists, we connected capsize to glacial earthquakes: at Greenland’s Helheim Glacier, calving drives a minutes-long reversal of the glacier’s flow and a downward flex of its terminus (Science 2015), and modeling the capsize force improves estimates of glacial-earthquake size (JGR 2021).

Ice mélange, the world’s largest granular material

In Greenland’s most productive fjords, calved icebergs and sea ice jam together into ice mélange. Combining terrestrial radar, satellite imagery, lab experiments and simulations, we showed that mélange flows like a granular material, with shear zones along the fjord walls where force chains between icebergs terminate. The resulting “buttressing” force on the glacier terminus can exceed 107 N per meter of width, enough to inhibit large calving events (PNAS 2018). At Jakobshavn Isbræ, calving is preceded by a loss of flow coherence in the mélange up to an hour in advance, pointing toward real-time detection of failure (Nature Geoscience 2021).

To isolate the essential mechanics, we built a scaled-down fjord filled with floating granular material. These experiments reveal stochastic rearrangements and fluctuations in mélange velocity and stress, even during slow, quasistatic flow (JGR Earth Surface 2025). With collaborators, we developed a mélange flow model based on nonlocal granular rheology (The Cryosphere 2025) and showed that mélange thickness at the terminus, which can be measured from space, provides a direct estimate of its buttressing force (Nature Communications 2025).

Ice rheology: from minutes to centuries

The slow creep of glacial ice plays a key role in sea-level rise, yet the standard description, Glen’s flow law, relates strain rate only to stress and cannot capture the transient creep observed when ice is first loaded. We developed a physics-based rheological model that reproduces all three regimes of transient creep in polycrystalline ice. It combines a series of elastic and viscous elements that produce Andrade-type power-law creep with a single viscous element whose viscosity evolves with the microstructure, representing reorientation of the ice grains. The model naturally captures the strain-rate minimum near 1% strain and the stress dependence of the transient phase, providing one framework for ice flow from minutes to centuries (preprint 2025).

In parallel, we use physics-informed neural networks to recover the nonlinear constitutive laws of non-Newtonian materials such as ice shelves. Trained on flow velocity and thickness from a laboratory-scale model of the glacier–ocean interface, these networks can identify rheologies that are difficult to measure directly.

Flowing, creeping and cracking

Creeping sand. Diffusing-wave spectroscopy lets us see strain deep inside a granular pile. A laser and optical filters create a speckle pattern across the sample, and correlating the speckle intensity over time reveals where and when grains rearrange. The video shows sand being poured into our imaging cell, with grains shifting and avalanching near the top. The same technique detects slow creep that continues for hours after a pile appears to be at rest, with implications for landslides and avalanches.

Hourglasses. An hourglass is a classic timer, yet its flow rate depends on more than the amount of sand and the size of the neck. When a timer is vented to the air, the number of vent holes, the ambient pressure and gas, and even the contact materials all change how quickly the sand drains.

Cracks in drying soils. Polygonal crack patterns appear in drying mud and paint. In drying suspensions of micron-sized particles, we found that the area of the polygons follows a universal power law, A ∝ h4/3, where h is the film thickness, and that cornstarch produces two generations of cracks: large polygons from capillary shrinkage, then smaller ones as the particles themselves deswell (PRE 2019).

The secret stickiness of sand

Ever wondered how a sand castle holds its shape? Water between the grains acts as a temporary glue, but a puzzle remains long after it dries: a fully dry cluster of sand can stay rigid. This phenomenon is everywhere, from crumbly clumps on a beach to the soil on other planets. We study the physics behind the persistent forces that hold grains together after the water is gone.

To find out why dry particles stick, we recreate these clusters in the lab using spherical glass beads as a clean, controlled model system. We wet the beads, dry them, and then measure how strong the resulting cluster is.

Wetting and drying. We immerse the beads in liquids of different polarity (water, ethanol, hexane and chloroform) and then evaporate the liquid in an oven. Another set of samples is treated with high-pressure steam in an autoclave before drying.

Measuring stickiness. A robotic arm with a force sensor pushes into each dried cluster. The force needed to break it apart quantifies the adhesion between the beads.

Steam makes strong bonds. High-pressure steam, from an autoclave or even an Instant Pot, produces by far the strongest clusters: humidity combined with pressure and temperature is very effective at bonding glass beads.

Cleaning the sand reveals a deeper puzzle. After four rounds of cleaning, steam-treated samples are only slightly stronger than untreated ones, yet beads simply wetted with pure water and dried remain significantly sticky. Our central question is: why the residual stickiness?

Because adhesion persists after extensive cleaning and without pressure, the liquid itself must change the bead surfaces. Polar liquids such as water create much stronger clusters than non-polar liquids such as hexane or chloroform, suggesting that liquid polarity drives surface chemistry changes that cause the residual adhesion.

Other mechanisms may also contribute. Could tiny, stable capillary bridges survive between grains even after a sample appears dry? Could electrostatic charge play a role? We are working to find out.
