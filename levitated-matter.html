Research

Levitated Matter

When particles float freely in air or plasma, there are no walls or substrates to add friction, and their interactions take center stage. In dusty plasmas and acoustic levitators those interactions are mediated by flowing ions or sound waves, so they are often nonreciprocal and non-pairwise, the same ingredients that drive active matter. We image these systems at high speed, track every particle in 2D or 3D, and infer the forces between them with single-particle resolution.

Dusty plasma

A dusty plasma is a weakly ionized gas seeded with micron-sized particles. The particles charge negatively and levitate in the plasma sheath above an electrode, where they can self-assemble into crystalline layers: a playground for lattice dynamics that we can watch one particle at a time. Because each particle sits in a stream of ions flowing toward the electrode at kilometers per second, it trails an ion wake, and the effective forces between particles become nonreciprocal: particle A can push on B harder than B pushes on A.

Learning forces from motion

Using laser-sheet tomography, we track small clusters of particles in 3D, with color in the videos marking each particle’s height. We then train machine-learning models whose structure builds in the physics of the problem, including its symmetries and the fact that no two particles are identical. This approach learns the nonreciprocal forces between particles with exquisite accuracy (R² > 0.99), measures every particle’s mass and charge, and revealed large deviations from standard theoretical assumptions: the plasma screening length grows with the size of the interacting particles, and the way particle charge scales with size changes with gas pressure. The work received the 2025 Cozzarelli Prize from PNAS. Building on it, the lab and Ilya Nemenman’s group are leading a DOE Genesis Mission project (2026) that uses AI to study the fluid dynamics of plasma, from the lab to the cosmos.

Nonreciprocity and intermittency

Driven, damped particle systems rarely settle into a steady state. Large crystals switch between a condensed state and a gaslike state on timescales from seconds to hours, without any bistable elements (PRL 2017). Differences in particle mass create localized vibrations that trigger transient “turbulent” puffs, captured by a single dimensionless number analogous to the Reynolds number for pipe flow (PRR 2020). Single particles can oscillate spontaneously with amplitudes exceeding 1 cm, which lets us read out the electric field and the charging time of the plasma sheath (PRR 2020). Most recently, we showed that nonreciprocal interactions between as few as two particles can pump energy into small Coulomb clusters, driving intermittent cycles of melting and recrystallization (Newton 2026). Acoustic levitation

A piezoelectric resonator and a glass plate create a standing sound wave that traps micron-scale particles in midair. At the resonance frequency, the particles assemble into a flat, two-dimensional raft. Driven above resonance, they become more energetic and spread into a three-dimensional “scattering state” in which they move freely, like a gas.

Between these extremes lies an “intermediate state.” Here the particles are mobile enough to exchange neighbors, yet still interact strongly through sound-mediated forces. When they collide, van der Waals forces can stick them together into structures such as polymer-like chains. By tracking particle identities, pairs and rotation angles, we measure how these sound-mediated, many-body interactions shape the collective behavior.

Electrostatics in the air

Static charge is everywhere in the atmosphere and in the natural world, yet its consequences are easy to overlook. An acoustic levitator can hold a single particle in midair for weeks. Coupled to a non-contact Faraday cup, it let us follow the charge on individual airborne particles over days: charge decays over about a week in dry air and over days in humid air, regardless of material or polarity, which suggests electrostatic forces can extend the time that coarse dust stays aloft (PNAS Nexus 2022).

Together with Victor Ortega-Jiménez (UC Berkeley), we are also exploring “electrostatic ecology.” Ticks can be pulled through the air toward charged hosts by electrostatic induction (Trends in Parasitology 2023). Parasitic nematodes that leap up to 25 times their body length rely on the static charge carried by flying insects to land on their hosts in midair; our models show that charge lowers the jump speed they need to succeed (PNAS 2025). The work was featured in The New York Times.

Levitated granular gases

Levitation can also remove friction from granular materials. Particles of solid CO₂ placed on a hot surface float on their own sublimated vapor, a Leidenfrost effect for solids, so they glide almost without friction. When two dense clusters of these particles collide, a “jamming front” sweeps through each cluster and removes more than 90% of the translational kinetic energy, with an initial energy loss that follows a power law in time, E ∝ t3/2. This work appeared on the cover of Physical Review Letters.
